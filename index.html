1. What Do the Sensors Actually Add?

Nexleaf's sensors are real and the data they generate is valuable. The question is marginal: valuable compared to what's already in place?

Remote temperature monitoring (RTM) works by continuously logging temperature and sending real-time alerts when an excursion happens. The health value it generates depends entirely on two things: (1) what monitoring was there before, and (2) whether someone can act on the alert. Both vary enormously across facilities and countries — which is why Nexleaf's headline numbers require careful reading.

The comparator ladder

Marginal value of adding a sensor falls monotonically as the existing monitoring gets better. Three rungs:

1
Nothing / paper thermometer Nexleaf's headline comparator
Manual spot-reads; excursions between reads are invisible. Largest possible RTM headroom. The 80% vaccine-loss reduction and 88% excursion-duration figures come from this comparison. This is not the baseline in Rwanda, Tanzania, or Ethiopia today.
2
30-day electronic recorder (30-DTR / Fridge-tag 2) The actual policy counterfactual
Continuous temperature log, on-device alarm, downloaded ~monthly at supervisory visits. Same sensor fidelity as RTM — the only thing it lacks is real-time notification. Present in most Gavi-funded facilities. Against this baseline, the Mozambique RCT shows RTM cuts freeze excursion duration by ~63% (not 80/88%), and only for the behavioural class where fast response is possible.
3
Existing RTM (any vendor) Rwanda's actual baseline since 2025
Real-time alerts already present. Adding ColdTrace where RTM already exists is RTM-on-RTM. Marginal value ≈ zero. Rwanda's entire fixed-facility cold chain was instrumented with ColdTrace by 2025.

What real-time alerting adds over a 30-DTR — and where it doesn't

A 30-DTR has identical sensor fidelity to ColdTrace. The only marginal contribution of real-time notification is speed of response. That speed advantage matters in one specific case: freeze or behavioural excursions, where the fix requires no part — re-stack vaccines, adjust a thermostat dial, switch to backup propane. A nurse can do this in minutes once alerted. Nexleaf's own data shows bare SMS alerts (no AI) reduced vaccine freezing in clinics by 74%.

For equipment failures — compressor, solar battery, PCB, refrigerant — faster detection sits upstream of the repair constraint. The alert arrives in real time; the fridge stays broken until a technician with the right part appears. In these cases the 30-DTR finding at the monthly download is functionally identical to the real-time alert: no one could have acted on either.

The two conditions for sensors to clear $300/DALY are in tension

Condition 1 (weak comparator): The facility would otherwise have only paper. Increasingly rare as Gavi CCEOP has equipped most target facilities with 30-DTRs. Condition 2 (high repair capacity): A functioning spare-parts and technician system converts detected faults into repaired uptime. This is exactly the mature system where Gavi's additionality is lowest. The two conditions rarely coexist.

2. What Does the AI Layer Add?

Nexleaf's AI troubleshooting assistant promises to turn any nurse into a de facto cold chain technician. The evidence tests that claim.

The AI case rests on a simple and appealing logic: most facilities have nurses; most facilities don't have technicians on-site; if AI can guide a nurse through a repair, you solve the availability problem without physically moving specialists. The question is whether the failures that actually keep fridges broken are the kind a nurse with guidance can fix.

What actually breaks — the failure-cause taxonomy

BucketExamplesAI + nurse resolvable?Share of failure events
A — Guide-onlyThermostat adjustment, condenser cleaning, door seal, defrost, overload resetYes~15–30% of events; less of downtime-days

D — InfrastructureGrid failure, voltage surge, solar panelOutside fridge scopeMajor in off-grid periphery

No LMIC study provides a clean A/B/C/D percentage split for vaccine refrigerators. The direction is unambiguous across every field source: B/C/D dominate, not A. The defensible estimate for Bucket A is 15–30% of failure events — and smaller still as a share of downtime-days, because specialist failures (C) wait the longest.

The key sensitivity: eM

The Key Sensitivity — eM

eM is the fraction of cold chain equipment downtime that an information-only tool can address without additional parts, a specialist, or a new budget line. The entire AI cost-effectiveness case turns on this number. Nexleaf verbally claimed eM ≈ 0.50. Every written source — including their own co-authored data — supports eM = 0.10–0.20. The difference is a 5× gap in $/DALY.

The three terms compound to a small number

Bucket A fraction (~15–30% of events) × fraction an unsupervised nurse can safely execute (a subset) × what AI adds over a bare SMS and phone-a-technician (small) = low-single-digit to low-teens percent of failure events where the AI adds net value — and less still as a share of downtime-days where B/C failures dominate.

Rwanda is Nexleaf's strongest reference case and its lowest-additional-value sensing investment. ColdTrace is already national at fixed facilities. The active frontier is the response leg: integrating ColdTrace alerts with MEMMS (the national maintenance management system), expanding to the new health-post tier targeting ≥600 facilities, and the spare-parts channels R3 built with Gavi.

At 92% baseline functionality, Rwanda has only 8pp of functionality headroom — the smallest of the three countries. The 100% CCE functionality result in Malawi required both sensors and a Rapid Response Fund. Sensors alone didn't get there; the material repair infrastructure did. Rwanda is a template for what maturity looks like once sensing is saturated. It is not an argument for adding more sensors.

Inverse headroom problem

Rwanda is the place where "parts partly solved via Gavi" has the most support — and where the marginal information layer buys the least. High system maturity and low marginal value move together.

Ethiopia has the largest genuine instrumentation headroom of the three countries — a large 30-DTR installed base with RTM at pilot scale, meaning new sensors face a real Rung-2 (not Rung-3) counterfactual at most facilities. This is where a ColdTrace deployment is most additional on the detection leg.

The tension: Ethiopia also has the thinnest and most centralised repair leg. Complex faults escalate to the Ministry of Health. The country is massive and heterogeneous — Addis Ababa vs. Afar, Somali, and Benishangul-Gumuz regions, where electricity and staffing are the primary constraints. The facilities where adding a sensor is most additional are also those where the response capacity is weakest. A sensor dropped into Ethiopia's most remote facilities without the repair system is a notification device with no one to respond.

Highest potential, most conditional

An Ethiopia deployment requires a baseline assessment before any CE claim. The opportunity is real — largest population, genuine 30-DTR counterfactual, Gavi-eligible — but only where repair capacity (parts, technicians, funding mechanism) is co-funded alongside the sensors.

Bottom Line

What the evidence collectively says — and the four questions that would change it.

On sensing: The marginal value of adding sensors is near-zero in Rwanda (already instrumented), small in Tanzania (confined to the un-instrumented peripheral tier, gated by the repair leg), and modest but conditional in Ethiopia (largest 30-DTR counterfactual, but thinnest repair leg). In no country does the realistic sensing case clear a $300/DALY bar against the honest counterfactual.

On AI uplift: The "nurse instead of engineer" use case is real only for the narrow behavioural/freeze slice — and there, plain SMS alerts already capture most of the win (74% reduction in freezing, no AI required). For the parts-and-specialist failures that dominate downtime-days, no guidance resolves what the facility doesn't have: the part, the technician, the budget. The binding constraint is material — confirmed by every field source, including Nexleaf's own co-authored failure analysis.

The best Nexleaf number is the maintenance institutionalization layer — $540–$940/DALY gross — because it directly supplies spare parts, technician capacity, and budget planning. Even that lands at $1,543–$2,687 once Gavi's additionality is applied, and ~7× GiveWell on a gross basis.

Four data requests that would change this assessment

The verbal claim that most downtime is informational would, if true, substantially improve the AI case. The data to test it exists in Nexleaf's own systems.

Specific requests before a funding decision
  1. The failure-cause breakdown from the Mozambique 83-fridge co-authored study — how do the 27 detected failures code against the "only 1 in 5 need a part" verbal claim?
  2. The source for "roughly half the failure burden is informational" — distinguishing diagnostic-knowledge gaps (AI-addressable) from coordination gaps (capturable by a rules engine, doesn't justify an LLM premium).
  3. Any measured outcome — fridge uptime or vaccine loss prevented, not tool adoption — attributable to the AI guide over a plain SMS alert plus phone-a-technician.
  4. A swap test: does the AI close downtime that a non-AI rules engine on the existing ColdTrace sensor stream would not? If not, Channel A coordination efficiency is available without the AI layer.
